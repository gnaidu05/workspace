# Running the $250 audit

*The Audit is three hours of work that produces one document. That document is
the only sales material the Build ever needs, so it is worth doing properly.*

---

## What the client is actually buying

Not your opinion. **A written description of their own process that they did
not have before.**

Most people running a broken spreadsheet have never seen the whole process
written down — they hold it in their heads, in fragments, across several
people. The single most valuable thing in the report is usually section 1,
where you just tell them what they do. That is what makes the rest credible.

## Before the call — 20 minutes

Ask for a copy of the sheet with real data in it (or anonymised, if they
prefer). Then, before speaking to anyone:

- **Count the tabs.** Note which are data, which are working, which are dead.
- **Find the formulas that reference other tabs.** These are the joints where
  it breaks.
- **Find the manual steps.** Anywhere a human retypes, copies, or "remembers to".
- **Find the dates.** How far back does it go? When did it stop being designed
  and start being accreted?

Arrive already knowing the shape of it. It changes the entire tone of the call.

## The call — 45 minutes

Nine questions. Do not send these in advance; the useful answers are the
unrehearsed ones.

1. **Walk me through a normal week with this sheet.** (Let them talk. Do not
   interrupt. This is most of the report.)
2. **Who touches it, and what does each person do to it?**
3. **What happens when two of you have it open at once?**
4. **What's the last thing that went wrong with it?**
5. **What do you do that you know is stupid, but works?**
6. **What would you check if you didn't trust the number it gave you?**
7. **What happens to this process if you're on holiday?**
8. **What would have to be true for this to be worth paying to fix?**
9. **Is there anything downstream of this — a report, an invoice, someone
   else's system — that depends on it?**

Question 5 produces the best answers in the whole list. Question 8 is the one
that tells you whether there is a Build here at all.

## The report — 90 minutes

Six sections. Plain language, no jargon, and short. A $250 document that runs
to thirty pages reads as padding; four to six pages reads as expertise.

### 1. How the process works today

Write their process back to them as a numbered sequence, naming who does each
step. Include the manual steps and the workarounds. **No commentary yet.**

They should read this and think *yes, that's exactly it* — possibly for the
first time ever.

### 2. Where it's fragile

Each item: what breaks, what triggers it, what it costs when it happens.
Ranked by likely damage, not by how interesting it is.

Be specific. "Cell references break when rows are inserted above line 400" is
useful. "The spreadsheet is fragile" is not.

### 3. What's worth automating

Ranked. For each: the effort, the benefit, and — honestly — whether it's worth
doing. Not everything is. Say so.

### 4. What you could do yourself, for free

**Do not skip this section.** There is almost always something: a named range,
data validation, protected sheets, a pivot table instead of nine formulas.

Giving away the free wins is what makes the paid recommendation believable.
A report that concludes "you should pay me for everything" reads like a sales
document, because it is one.

### 5. If you build it

Scope, price, timeline, the hour cap. Exactly what would exist at the end, and
what would not. This is the Build quote — but it sits in section 5 of a
document that has already been useful, rather than arriving as a pitch.

### 6. If you do nothing

What this costs over the next year, in hours and in risk. Not a scare tactic —
an honest figure, and sometimes a small one. If doing nothing is genuinely the
right call, say that, and you will get the referral instead.

## Rules

1. **Never recommend a build you wouldn't buy.** You will spend $250 of
   credibility to gain $1,500, once, and then have neither.
2. **If a no-code tool solves it, say so.** They paid for an answer, not an
   upsell. The FAQ on the offer page promises exactly this — keep the promise.
3. **Send the report whatever happens.** Even if they have gone quiet, even if
   you've concluded there is no Build. It is theirs; they paid for it.
4. **Quote the Build in the report, once.** Then stop. No follow-up sequence.
5. **Keep the report.** Anonymised, it becomes the sample that sells the next
   audit.

## Why the free-advice section pays

The counter-intuitive part: sections 4 and 6 — where you give away fixes and
sometimes advise doing nothing — are what make section 5 land.

The client has just watched you argue against your own interest twice. When
you then say a build is worth it, there is no reason left to doubt you. That is
worth far more than the occasional $1,500 you talk yourself out of.

## Time budget

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-call review | 20 min |
| The call | 45 min |
| Writing | 90 min |
| Buffer | 25 min |
| **Total** | **3 hours → Rs 7,260/hr** |

Past four hours, an Audit stops being worth doing. If it consistently runs
over, the questions are wrong, not the price.
